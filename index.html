The real epiphany: it's not the 401k, it's the brokerage rate
Every young surgeon already knows to max the 401k/403(b) and backdoor Roth every year — that part is basically automatic and capped by law. Hospital-employed surgeons usually also have a 457(b), another $24,500 pre-tax that many never use. Fewer know about cash balance / defined benefit plans, which some private groups offer and which can shelter a large additional amount pre-tax depending on age and group structure — worth asking your group's financial advisor or CPA about explicitly, since it isn't always volunteered. But the single biggest lever after that, the one actually within your control every single year, is your taxable brokerage savings rate. Tax-advantaged accounts are capped. The taxable brokerage does not. Pay yourself first, monthly, on autopilot — treat the taxable brokerage contribution like a bill that's due before lifestyle spending happens, and raise that automated amount every time pay increases or a big fixed cost ends. When daycare ends, when loans are paid off — redirect that exact amount the same month, before it gets absorbed.

Lifestyle creep and the 12-month rule
Lifestyle creep doesn't feel like a decision — it feels like finally affording normal things. It is the main reason high-earning physicians aren't wealthy. The 12-month rule: when income rises (attending pay, a raise, a bonus, a cost that ends), keep spending flat for 12 months and send the difference to savings. After a year you won't miss what you never started spending, and the higher savings rate feels normal. Made once at the residency-to-attending transition, this decision is worth more than any investment choice you'll ever make.
Plan for the expensive years you can see coming: parental leave (often partly unpaid for surgeons), fertility treatment, a new car, a wedding. Fund them from a separate savings bucket so they don't come out of the brokerage transfer.
Size fixed costs to guaranteed pay. Bonuses can shrink or vanish with reimbursement cuts, a bad year, or a job change. The mortgage and tuition don't. If the house only works with the bonus, it's too much house.

      How to invest it — the three-fund portfolio
      Keep it simple. You don't need to pick stocks, time the market, or pay anyone to do it for you. Own the whole market, cheaply, and leave it alone.
      • Three low-cost index funds: a US total stock market fund, an international total stock market fund, and a US total bond market fund. That's the whole portfolio.
      • Mostly stocks while you're young — they're what grows. Add bonds gradually as retirement gets closer, so a crash late in your career can't derail you.
      • Keep costs low. Total-market index funds at Vanguard, Fidelity and Schwab cost a few hundredths of a percent a year.
      • Automate it — the same funds, every month, in every account.
      • Rebalance once a year back to your mix. Ignore the news the rest of the year.
      • Never sell in a crash. Crashes are normal; selling is what makes them permanent.
      • Skip the 1% advisor. On $2M that's $20,000 a year, forever. If you want help, pay a flat fee.
      To go deeper: The Simple Path to Wealth (JL Collins) and the Bogleheads wiki's "Three-fund portfolio" page.

      The simple math — how much you need, and why your savings rate matters most
      1. How much do I need? The 4% rule
      1. Where it comes from. In the 1990s, researchers tested every historical stretch of the US market — including the Great Depression and the inflation of the 1970s — to see how much retirees could safely spend.
      2. What it says. Take 4% of your portfolio in your first year of retirement, raise that dollar amount with inflation every year after, and historically the money lasted at least 30 years.
      3. The arithmetic. Divide what you'll spend each year by 4% — the same as multiplying by 25.
      4. The fine print. Retiring before about 55 means a longer retirement, so a more cautious 3.5% (×28.6) is reasonable: . Social Security at 70 isn't counted and adds a cushion later.
      2. Why your savings rate matters more than your salary
      Your savings rate works on both ends at once. Every dollar you save is a dollar invested and a dollar you've learned to live without — so the number you need shrinks too. Salary helps, but only through what you save: a raise that turns into a bigger house and a nicer car raises your number as fast as your savings.       When does work become optional?

      Work optional = saved enough that you could stop saving and still retire on time. From then on your paycheck only has to cover today's bills — so you can leave a bad job, go part-time, or pay for call coverage. Some people call it FU money.

      What changed in 2026
      SAVE — eliminated. Struck down by federal courts. Borrowers moved to IBR or interest-only forbearance. No reinstatement expected.
      RAP — new July 1, 2026. Repayment Assistance Plan. 1% of total AGI at $10–20k, rising 1 point per $10k bracket to 10% above $100k; minus $50/month per dependent; $10 minimum. No payment cap. Qualifies for PSLF.
      IBR — still available. 10% of discretionary income (AGI minus 150% of the poverty line for your family size), never more than the 10-year standard payment. Usually lower than RAP for high-earning attendings. Qualifies for PSLF.
      PSLF — intact. 120 qualifying payments at a nonprofit / government employer → 100% tax-free forgiveness. Best deal in medicine if your employer qualifies.
      Non-PSLF IDR forgiveness — now taxable. Carrying loans 20–25 years on IBR/RAP without PSLF now triggers a taxable forgiveness event. For a surgeon, that could mean a $200–600k+ tax bill in retirement. The old "low payment, wait it out" strategy is now a trap.
      Rates 2026–27: 8.07% for graduate unsubsidized loans, 9.07% for Grad PLUS (fixed at disbursement). Grad PLUS is closed to new borrowers after July 1, 2026; new borrowing is capped. Slider defaults to 8.1%.
      Fixed costs are the decision that decides everything else
      A new attending salary feels unlimited, so a $4,000/month house upgrade or a $2,000/month lease feels small. It isn't. It is paid from take-home, not gross. Every dollar needs almost two dollars of salary. It never goes away when income dips. And it hits twice: it is money not saved and it raises the amount you need before you can stop working.
